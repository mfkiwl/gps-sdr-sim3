Two samples per chip.
What can you reconstruct?

You are right that 2.5 MS/s gives about 2.44 samples per GPS C/A chip. The missing piece is what you mean by restore the chip: its sign, its exact edge, or a filtered waveform.

Why this matters at your USRP

You choose --rate 2500000, generate I/Q samples, and let the X300 produce RF. It is tempting to conclude that every detail of the original rectangular chips must survive because the sample rate exceeds twice the chip rate.

That conclusion mixes two jobs: recovering a sequence of +1/−1 chip values, and reproducing a continuous waveform between sample times. A receiver may do the first successfully while the transmitted edges are rounded and some spectral detail is absent.

This distinction helps you decide what a higher sample rate might improve. It can change waveform fidelity. It does not automatically add navigation information or prove more accurate absolute RF timing.

01 / values versus waveform

The samples do not say where this edge was

Drag the orange edge between the two sample times. Watch the black sample dots and the list below. Can they tell you which edge position was the original?

The last positive sample is at 0.8 µs. The first negative one is at 1.2 µs. Every edge position between them gives the same samples. You know the two signs, but these samples alone do not locate the exact transition.

Knowing the PRN sequence, chip clock and a signal model can add constraints. Receivers use that knowledge and many observations to estimate timing, potentially below one sample interval. This drawing is not a claim that GPS timing accuracy is limited to 400 ns. It shows why samples alone need assumptions for unique reconstruction.

02 / why edges need bandwidth

A sharp chip contains fast variations

Start with the smooth blue curve. Increase the number of sine components. The chip duration stays fixed; the edges become sharper because you add faster components.

One complete +1/−1 cycle takes two chips, so the first sine is at 0.5115 MHz. The next is at 1.5345 MHz, then 2.5575 MHz, and so on. The chip rate stays 1.023 Mchip/s throughout.

Those added frequencies are harmonics. A perfect vertical edge needs indefinitely high frequencies. Its bandwidth is therefore not limited to the chip rate. The small ripples near each edge are a property of this truncated sine series, not measurement noise.

The “two samples” rule counts the fastest retained sinusoidal component, not chips. Chip rate tells you how often the symbol can change. Bandwidth tells you which rates of variation exist in the waveform.

03 / the missing assumption

Two different waves can give exactly the same samples

Both curves pass through every black dot, even beyond this visible window. Move the blue frequency. The orange frequency always stays 2.5 MHz higher. Sampling remains fixed at 2.5 MS/s.

This is aliasing: different continuous waveforms become indistinguishable at the sample times. At the default setting, the samples cannot distinguish 0.5 MHz from 3 MHz unless we already exclude one of them.

Now impose a rule: the original low-pass waveform contains no frequencies at or above 1.25 MHz in magnitude. The orange candidate is no longer allowed. That kind of bandwidth restriction makes unique reconstruction possible.

For a low-pass waveform with highest retained frequency B:
sample rate Fs > 2B

That is the Nyquist–Shannon condition used here. In the ideal theorem, uniformly spaced, exact samples over the full time axis determine the band-limited waveform. Actual hardware uses finite filters and quantized samples, so reconstruction is approximate.

The identical-samples argument is illustrated in Charles Boncelet’s sampling lesson; the reconstruction condition is explained in Steven W. Smith’s sampling theorem chapter.

Why not say exactly two samples per cycle?

At the boundary, phase matters: sampling sin(2π × 1.25 MHz × t) at 2.5 MS/s gives zero at every sample. That looks like no signal. We use a strict inequality and leave a transition band for real filters.

04 / your actual numbers

2.5 MS/s fits the main lobe, not the whole ideal spectrum

Move the sample-rate slider. The shaded interval is the frequency range a centered complex I/Q stream can represent without wrapping. Notice that the GPS spectrum stays where it is while the interval expands.

At your setting, the main lobe between −1.023 and +1.023 MHz fits inside −1.25 to +1.25 MHz. The smaller lobes outside that interval do not disappear from the ideal rectangular-chip spectrum.

The first spectral zero is not a hard cutoff. That is why treating “1.023 Mchip/s” as “nothing above 1.023 MHz” gives the wrong guarantee.

C/A timing comes from the GPS interface specification. The plotted envelope is sinc²(f/Rc), the rectangular-chip BPSK model derived in ESA Navipedia’s BPSK explanation.

Why does 2.046 MHz total width fit in 2.5 MS/s? Should I double it again?

Here B = 1.023 MHz is the maximum magnitude of baseband frequency, so 2B = 2.046 MHz is already the two-sided width. A centered complex stream at Fs spans −Fs/2 to +Fs/2, a total width of Fs. Each I/Q pair supplies two real values at each sample time. Do not double the two-sided width again.

Where does the chip rate calculation fit?

One chip lasts 1 / 1.023 MHz ≈ 977.5 ns. One sample at 2.5 MS/s spans 400 ns. The ratio is about 2.44 samples per chip. For direct rectangular-code evaluation, individual chips generally occupy two or three sample instants. This ratio describes sampling density. It is not a proof of exact continuous-waveform reconstruction.

The corrected statement: “2.5 MS/s can represent a suitably band-limited GPS C/A baseband waveform covering the main lobe. Having 2.44 samples per chip does not prove perfect reproduction of ideal rectangular chips.”

05 / what your computer sends

The USRP reconstructs I/Q, not a hidden chip list

Your transmitter is synthesizing a waveform. There is no original analog chip waveform being measured first. The computer chooses I/Q values; the USRP’s interpolation and analog chain turn those values into RF.

For one idealized satellite, with amplitude A = 10,000, data d = +1, chip c = −1 and phase φ = 0, the sample is (I, Q) = (−10000, 0). At phase π/2 it is (0, −10000). These numbers already contain the chip’s sign.

The normal generator in player/x300tx.cpp, around lines 467–507, evaluates the code and carrier at each sample time, sums contributions and clips to int16. The stream uses sc16 on both host and wire around line 3144. That loop is not an ideal anti-alias filter applied to a continuous rectangular waveform.

The shaded spectrum in the previous demo is a representable interval, not a filter that the player automatically applies. Directly evaluating ideal chip steps at a finite rate can introduce sampling artifacts. Filtering after that sampling cannot recover distinctions already lost. Higher-rate synthesis and deliberate filtering can control this, but that would be a separate implementation choice.

The 1575.42 MHz carrier is added by the radio’s RF chain. It is not the B in the baseband inequality above. A higher I/Q rate also does not make the existing chip code run faster: the player uses the actual rate to advance model time.

Where this shows up in your experiments

When a receiver tracks the 2.5 MS/s signal, that demonstrates usable code and navigation information under those conditions. It does not demonstrate that every ideal chip edge or sidelobe was reproduced.

When comparing 2.5 and 5 MS/s, look for changes in measured spectrum and receiver correlation or tracking, with the rest of the experiment controlled. Receiver bandwidth may remove the extra spectrum anyway. Sampling density, receiver timing precision and calibrated absolute RF delay are different quantities.

Explain why: “the receiver recovers the chips” and “the edges are rounded” can both be true

Chip detection asks which code signs best explain the received signal, using timing and a code model. Exact waveform reconstruction asks for all continuous values between samples. Filtering can round transitions while preserving enough correlation information to identify the code and decode data. The two tasks require different information.

Remember the two questions: Can I identify the chips? and Which continuous waveform do these samples uniquely specify? Nyquist–Shannon answers the second under a bandwidth assumption.
